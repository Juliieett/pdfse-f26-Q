## 1. Interviews I personally asked

### Interview 04 — Avto Vani: Considering a new crop

[Read interview log](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/04-farmer-avto.md)

Avto mainly grows crops he already knows how to take care of. He has considered planting a new crop, such as orange, but is unsure whether it would work on his land. He asks other farmers for advice and searches online, although he does not find every online recommendation relevant to his conditions.

**Evidence:** “I want to plant a new crop, but I don't know how well it will do on my land.” He also explained that not every piece of online advice suits his land.

### Interview 15 — Nadia: Treatment schedules and product selection

[Read interview log](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/13-nadia-farmer)

Nadia is involved in farming and described two difficulties: sometimes forgetting when plants need treatment and not knowing exactly which agricultural product to use. She sometimes asks other people for advice.

**Evidence:** “Sometimes I forget when I need to treat the plants.” She also said that she does not know exactly which product she should use.

**My learning:** I initially might have treated this as one problem that could be solved with reminders. The interview suggests that there are two separate issues: remembering when to act and knowing what action or product is appropriate. A reminder alone would not necessarily solve the second issue.

**Next question:** “Tell me about the last time you forgot a treatment or were unsure which product to use. What did you do next, and what happened?”

### What these interviews taught me

The two interviews concern related agricultural decisions, but they do not establish the same problem. Avto described uncertainty about whether a new crop would suit his land. Nadia described remembering treatment dates and selecting suitable products. I should not combine these into one pattern just because both interviewees are farmers.

I also need to distinguish reported difficulties from proven consequences. The interviews suggest potential financial and crop-related costs, but I should ask for specific past examples before claiming that a particular loss occurred.

## 2. Pattern journal — Weeks 1–4

### Week 1 — Start with a problem, not a solution

**Evidence reviewed:** [Problem pool](../../00-foundation/problem-pool.md)

My main lesson is that an interesting idea is not automatically a validated problem. Before deciding what to build, I need to understand who experiences the difficulty, what they are trying to accomplish, and what they currently do about it.

For Nergi, this means I should not begin by assuming that farmers need an application. I need to establish the actual situation in which a farmer struggles and what makes the existing way of dealing with it insufficient.

**What I would test next:** Ask people to describe a recent real experience instead of asking whether they like a proposed solution.

### Week 2 — Evaluate problems using evidence

**Evidence reviewed:** [Four-filters scorecard](../../00-foundation/four-filters-scorecard.md) and [team decisions](../../DECISIONS.md)

The four filters provide a way to compare problem ideas rather than choosing whichever sounds most interesting. A useful problem should matter to the person, be reachable through interviews, allow a small test, and have evidence of a paying workaround or other meaningful cost.

This changes how I evaluate agricultural problems. A problem can sound serious without being frequent, expensive, or important enough for someone to change their behavior. I need to ask what people already do, what it costs them, and whether they have tried to solve it.

**What I would test next:** Look for a recent example and identify the workaround, the effort or money involved, and the result.

### Week 3 — Test predictions and pay attention to contradictions

**Evidence reviewed:** [Team decisions](../../DECISIONS.md), including the recorded prediction checks for the new-crop problem.

The evidence about growing unfamiliar crops made me question the broad assumption that farmers simply cannot find advice. The more specific possibility is that advice exists but may be unreliable, conflicting, or difficult to verify before spending money.

The Manana interview provides a concrete example: she bought 60 kiwi plants from a seller, did not know what she was buying, and waited two years without fruit from the first batch. She later bought another batch from a seller who explained the male/female ratio. This is stronger evidence of a costly experience than a general statement that agricultural information is hard to find.

**My caution:** Manana’s experience is team evidence, not an interview I personally asked. I should use it to compare findings, but not count it as one of my own interviews.

**What I would test next:** Ask another grower to describe the last time they bought planting material or tried a new crop, including how they selected the seller and what they learned only after planting.

### Week 4 — Separate patterns from individual stories

**Evidence reviewed:** [Avto interview](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/04-farmer-avto.md), [Nadia interview](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/13-nadia-farmer), [Manana interview](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/10-farmer-racha-manana.md), and [team decisions](../../DECISIONS.md).

The interviews show different kinds of uncertainty in farming. Avto is unsure whether an unfamiliar crop will suit his land. Nadia sometimes forgets treatment dates and is unsure which products to use. Manana experienced a costly problem with planting material and missing information about the male/female ratio.

These cases may all involve agricultural decision-making, but that broad similarity does not prove they share one root cause. The most concrete cost in the evidence currently available is Manana’s experience with the first batch of kiwi plants. Avto’s potential losses and Nadia’s potential treatment-related losses still need to be established with specific examples.

**My learning:** I should keep a pattern only when multiple interviews support the same underlying problem, not merely the same general topic. I should also record evidence that weakens my initial prediction rather than selecting only details that support it.

**What I would test next:** Investigate how growers verify the quality and suitability of planting material before purchase, including what information they receive from sellers and whether they have any independent way to check it.

## 3. Mom Test reflection

The most useful questions focus on past behavior rather than hypothetical interest.

Questions such as “Would you use an app that reminds you about treatments?” or “Would you like a tool that tells you which crop to grow?” would encourage people to agree with an appealing idea without proving that the problem is important.

Instead, I should ask about the last time the problem happened, what the person did, whom they asked for help, what they spent, and what the result was. I should avoid explaining Nergi’s proposed solution before understanding the existing behavior.

For example, with Nadia, I should establish how she currently remembers treatment dates, what she does when she is unsure which product to use, and whether either situation has caused a real consequence. With Avto, I should ask about an actual attempt to grow an unfamiliar crop rather than assuming that considering a new crop means he has already experienced a failed harvest.

## 4. Current conclusion

My current learning is that agricultural problems should be investigated at the level of a specific decision and its consequences. “Farmers need better information” is too broad to guide a useful first prototype.

The evidence raises at least three distinct questions:

1. How do growers determine whether planting material is suitable and reliable before buying it?
2. How do farmers decide which treatment or agricultural product to use?
3. How do growers determine whether an unfamiliar crop will suit their land?

The first question currently has the clearest documented example of a specific financial cost in the team’s interview evidence. It is a candidate for further validation, not proof that every grower has the same problem.

My next step is to interview more growers about recent purchases of planting material, focusing on how they chose the seller, what information they received before paying, and whether they could verify that information independently.
